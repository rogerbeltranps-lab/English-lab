## 0. Rules that apply to every page

- **Routes are cumulative.** Item tags: **[I]** all routes, **[S]** Safe and Daring, **[D]** Daring only. Insecure instructions and feedback are in Catalan; English stays the target language.
- **Recall warm-up** opens every learning path (LP): 6–10 short items, typed answers where possible, no notes and no vocabulary visible until the student submits. Feedback shows the answer and where to review it. It counts as done when submitted (low stakes), not by score.
- **Accuracy rule:** auto-corrected steps (practice, reading, listening, grammar, mixed practice) complete at **≥ 80 %**, rounded up (e.g. 5/6, 8/10). Students can retry with new feedback.
- **Mixed practice** (interleaving) appears after each grammar point is taught. Items mix the new form with earlier ones, so students have to *choose* the form.
- **Flashcards:** the existing 10-card random set. The deck is the current LP's vocabulary **plus the earlier LS2 LPs** (spaced review).
- **Production:** every LP ends with a writing or speaking task with a checklist, AI feedback (existing ChatGPT button) and a revision. The text is saved as a **product piece** and shown again on the Final Product page.
- Religious meanings are presented as information ("For many Hindus…"), never as belief. Students choose which tradition they present.

### Reading: *Four celebrations, four seasons*

> **Diwali.** Diwali is the festival of lights. Hindus, Sikhs and Jains celebrate it in October or November, and it lasts five days. People clean and decorate their homes, light small oil lamps called diyas and make colourful patterns on the floor. Families visit relatives, exchange gifts and share sweets. In many cities there are fireworks at night. For many people, the lights mean that good wins over evil and light wins over darkness.
>
> **Hanukkah.** Hanukkah is a Jewish festival. It usually takes place in December and lasts eight nights. Every night, families light one more candle on a menorah, so on the last night there are eight candles. People eat food cooked in oil, like potato pancakes and doughnuts, and children play a game with a spinning top called a dreidel.
>
> **Lunar New Year.** Lunar New Year is celebrated in China, Vietnam, Korea and many other places in late January or February. Before the festival, people clean their houses to sweep away bad luck. On New Year's Eve, families get together for a big reunion dinner, and many people travel home for the holidays. The streets are full of red lanterns, and there are parades with dragon and lion dances. Children get red envelopes with money.
>
> **Castanyada.** In Catalonia, the Castanyada is held on 31 October, the night before All Saints' Day. Families and friends get together to roast chestnuts and sweet potatoes and eat panellets. In some schools, a student dresses up as a castanyera, an old woman who sells chestnuts in the street.

### Grammar

### Reading: *Check it before you share it*

> Before you put information on a slide, check it. Ask four questions.
>
> **1. Who wrote it?** Look for an author or an organisation, for example a museum, a university, an encyclopedia or an official tourism website. If you can't find who wrote it, be careful.
>
> **2. When was it written or updated?** The dates of some festivals change every year, especially festivals that follow the moon, like Diwali, Hanukkah and Lunar New Year. Check that the information is for this year.
>
> **3. Why was it written?** Some pages want to inform you. Others want to sell you something, like costumes or trips, or just want lots of views. A shop may say that a tradition is "ancient" because it sounds good.
>
> **4. Do other sources say the same?** Never trust only one source. Find each fact in at least two reliable sources. If they don't agree, choose the more reliable one or say that the sources disagree.
>
> When you explain a religious celebration, present it as information, not as belief: "Hindus believe that…", "For many Jewish families, the candles mean…".
>
> Finally, credit your sources: write the title of the page, the website or author, and the year. Don't copy whole sentences. Take notes with key words and then write your own sentences.
